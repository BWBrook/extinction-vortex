# Case study: the Haley Lake marmot colony (draft manuscript text)

Fable, 14 October 2026. This is the descriptive deep dive under
`vortex_haley_deep_dive_protocol_20261014.md`.
- Figure: `analysis/fable/haley/haley_case.{png,pdf}`.
- Tables: `analysis/fable/haley/source_table_{annual,transitions}.csv`.
- Register: `analysis/fable/haley/conflict_register.md` (entries A1–A7,
  B1–B6, C).

It contains no test, fitted reconstruction or new interval.

---

One of the nine Fagan–Holmes series, the Vancouver Island marmot (*Marmota
vancouverensis*) count in Williams et al. (2021, ID 15), can be matched to a
single natural colony with an unusually detailed field record: Haley Lake,
one of five colonies studied intensively by Bryant (1998). The Williams series
falls from 33 animals in 1981 to 0 in 2000 (Fig. Xa). For 1988–1997, the
colony's age-summed annual totals in Bryant's Appendix 7 equal the Williams
values exactly. They differ in 1987, where Bryant gives 18 against 28 (register
A1). No source was recovered for 1981–1986 or 1998–2000, including the
terminal zero (B1). The case therefore illustrates both what a matched field
record adds to an extinction-bound count series and how far it remains from a
complete demographic account.

**What the count is.** Bryant's annual figure is a sum of age-class seasonal
maxima, not a single-day census (p. 14). At intensively studied colonies it
includes all marked animals seen during the season, which need not all have
been present at once (p. 37). Effort rose after 1992. One visit detected about
44% of tagged adults across colonies (p. 34), and correction factors were not
applied to individual colony-years (p. 15). The series is a carefully
collected index of animals present during each season, with unquantified,
possibly changing detection.

**What the extra record shows.** Three features stand out that the aggregate
count conceals.
- **Adults declined.** Animals aged two and over fell from 12–14 in
  1987–1990 to 4–7 in 1994–1997 (Fig. Xb).
- **Reproduction was episodic.** Litters were recorded in 8 of 11 years,
  between 0 and 4 a year, with none in 1989, 1992 or 1995 (Fig. Xd). Pup
  counts accordingly swing between 0 and 12, and reproduction was concentrated
  among a few females (Appendix 14).
- **The colony was open.** Nine immigrants were recorded over the decade
  (Fig. Xd). Bryant notes that immigration enabled breeding in 1988 (p. 47).
  Emigration cannot be estimated from the thesis, but a tagged movement from
  Haley to Bell Creek is documented.

**Persistence of marked animals.**
- **Non-pups.** Of 97 recorded annual exposures of tagged non-pups (adults
  and yearlings), 64 were seen again as adults the following year (Fig. Xc).
  Annual values range from 4 of 9 to 3 of 3.
- **Pups.** Of 24 tagged-pup exposures, 11 were seen as yearlings the
  following year. None of the tagged pups in the 1991 and 1996 cohorts was
  observed as a yearling in the following year (0 of 3 and 0 of 7).

These are minimum persistence rates: missed animals, lost tags and emigrants
count as losses (p. 18). The exposures are not independent animals, and we
attach no intervals.

**Where the record does not hold together.** The tables do not form a
consistent ledger.
- For the 1994→1995 transition, 10 tagged adults are recorded as surviving,
  yet only 7 adults appear in the 1995 total (A3).
- 1996 has 7 tagged pups in one table and 4 in another (A4).
- Totals disagree between tables and text (A5, A6).
- The thesis gives two transplant accounts, Mount McQuillan and Franklin.
  Their linkage is unresolved, the donor colony is unknown, and no Haley
  removal is coded (A7).

We keep these conflicts visible rather than resolving them arithmetically;
their causes (transcription, revision or different inclusion rules) are
unresolved.

**What the case can and cannot tell us.** The Haley record supplies exactly
the kinds of information the count-only procedure lacked: age structure,
marked-animal persistence, reproduction and movement. It shows a colony whose
decline coincided with fewer adults, episodic reproduction and recorded
immigration, including a breeding episode that Bryant attributed to
immigration. It does not isolate abundance-dependent feedback.
- The rates were measured during the decline, so they may reflect feedback,
  external drivers such as predation or habitat change, movement, changing
  observation, or chance.
- They are not an independent estimate of a no-feedback baseline.
- The final disappearance has no recovered source here and is not a
  documented mortality event.

A formal feedback analysis of this colony would need more than the
nine-population estimator relabelled. It would need a case-specific estimand,
a mapping from seasonal maxima to the model's census, bounded source
conflicts, and models of movement and of the dependence between the marked
and counted records. It would then need its own calibration and validation.
None of this is attempted here. The case's contribution is to show concretely
why a declining count, even one matched to a well-studied colony, does not by
itself reveal the mechanism of its decline. It also shows what kinds of
demographic evidence would be needed to do better.
